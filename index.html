Before we set off

You build. You obsess over details. You see around corners. And you sense what people actually need. That's an inventor with a strong business sense.

Jana, before we talk about subjects, I want to talk about you. You like to make things, and you make them properly. You notice what other people walk right past. You're creative, you're seriously smart, and people simply like being around you.

I'm not going to tell you what to study. That's your decision. What I can give you is a look at five fields that will shape the next fifty years: what's already real, what's still unsolved, and what someone like you could build. Then you pick the one that won't let go of you.

Rule one

Follow what makes you forget dinner

You know the feeling. You start on something after school, and suddenly it's dark outside and someone has called you for dinner twice. That's the feeling to look for. You're smart enough for almost anything, so the question isn't what's in demand. It's what you would do even if nobody made you. Boredom keeps you at the surface. Fascination pulls you deep.

Rule two

Pack tools that don't rust

Software tools rust fast. The laws of nature never do. AI is taking over the routine parts of science. What stays human is the question nobody has asked yet and the bridge between two worlds. AI doesn't replace these subjects. It makes the people who understand them faster.

Rule three

The map has changed

The crossroads you'll travel for the next 50 years isn't the one we studied for over 20 years ago, when Wirtschaftsinformatik looked like the future. The old map shows roads that are already overgrown. So look forward, not back.

The bridge

Inventor and founder

Here's something most people learn too late. The person who invents something and the person who brings it into the world are usually two different people. The rare ones are both.

And here is the part nobody tells you. When something truly fascinates you, other people want in. They join you, they back you, they buy from you. Great inventions only change the world when someone builds a company around them. In these fields, those founders are almost always scientists and engineers, because nobody else understands the invention well enough.

I've started companies myself. Here's what I learned. Business sense you can pick up along the way, from customers, from mistakes, from doing it. Hard science you can only learn properly at university. So study the hard thing, and grow the business sense right next to it. You already have more of it than you think.

  1. Study the hard science.

    That's your unfair advantage. It's the part competitors can't copy.

  2. Find the founders on campus.

    Big technical universities have founder centers, like UnternehmerTUM in Munich. Show up from your first semester.

  3. Use the EXIST grant.

Robotics gives all of it hands. And energy is the fire that makes everything run. Every chapter has a window you can touch, a few true stories, the big problems nobody has solved yet, and a treasure map of companies the world is still waiting for.

Chapter I · Physics

The Deep and the Dark

Eleven kilometers down, in total darkness, something is alive next to water hotter than an oven. How did we ever find it?

Picture a submarine the size of a car, sinking into black water. At 200 meters the last sunlight is gone. At 4,000 meters the pressure would crush a soda can flat in a heartbeat. And at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, almost eleven kilometers down, a chimney of rock is blowing out water hotter than an oven, while strange creatures drift past it as if nothing were unusual.

Now turn around and go the other way. Up through the waves, past the clouds, through the green curtain of the aurora, out to where a telescope with golden mirrors floats in the dark, catching light that left its galaxy long before the Earth was born. Every machine on that trip was built by people who understood physics.

Let's go down first.

From the deepest point on Earth to the James Webb Space Telescope, 1.5 million km out. The gauge on the left tracks where you are. Move across it. Like real plankton, the deep sea lights up where you touch it.

Why it's your kind of adventure

Only about a quarter of the seafloor has been mapped in detail. We know the surface of the Moon better than our own oceans. Whoever understands physics builds the instruments for the unknown. And in instrument building, one tiny detail decides whether a telescope sees sharply or a sensor hears anything at all. That's exactly where you shine.

True stories
  • 400 °C

    On the seafloor, water shoots out of hydrothermal vents at up to 400 degrees and still doesn't boil, because the pressure is too high. Around it lives life that has never seen the Sun.

  • 10−19 m

    The LIGO detectors notice when their arms change length by less than a ten-thousandth of a proton. That's how we heard two black holes collide, over a billion light-years away.

  • 1.5 million km

    That's how far out the James Webb telescope floats. It sees galaxies whose light set off about 300 million years after the Big Bang.

Still unsolved

Problems still waiting for someone

These problems are so hard that whoever solves one changes physics textbooks and the world economy at the same time.

  1. A superconductor that works at room temperature

    Today's superconductors carry electricity with zero loss, but only when cooled to brutal cold. One that works at normal temperature would mean power lines that lose nothing, cheap MRI scanners for every clinic and trains that float. Many have claimed it. Nobody has proven it.

  2. A quantum computer that really works

    Quantum computers could simulate molecules no normal computer ever could, and design medicines and materials from scratch. Today they make too many errors. Building one that runs reliably is one of the great engineering races of this century.

  3. A warning before the earthquake

    Earthquakes still hit without warning and kill tens of thousands of people in a bad year. Nobody can predict them yet. New sensors, satellites and AI may finally hear what the Earth is about to do.

Companies the world still needs

Chapter II · Chemistry

The Alchemist's Workshop

Somewhere in the Arizona desert, a box of crystals is pulling drinking water out of air so dry it cracks your lips.

For centuries, alchemists tried to turn lead into gold. They failed, of course. But their great-great-grandchildren pulled off something stranger. They learned to build crystals so full of tiny rooms that a single gram hides the floor space of a soccer field. Leave one out in the Arizona desert overnight, and by morning it has caught drinking water out of thin, dry air.

That's chemistry today. You don't memorize the periodic table. You treat it like a box of building blocks that nobody has finished playing with.

Watch one of those crystals build itself.

A metal-organic framework (MOF) assembles itself in 3D, then traps CO₂ and water in its pores. Drag to turn the crystal.

Why it's your kind of adventure

Chemistry rewards people who love working with their hands and who are precise to the last decimal. A lab is a workshop where the raw material is matter itself. Computers now suggest millions of new molecules. Someone still has to pick the right one and make it real.

True stories
  • Nobel Prize 2025

    Awarded for these crystals, called metal-organic frameworks. They can pull water out of desert air and catch CO₂ from chimneys.

  • 7,000 m²

    That's how big the inner surface of a single gram can be. A soccer field in a teaspoon.

  • 2.2 million

    New crystal structures dreamed up by an AI from Google DeepMind. A robot lab in Berkeley then made 41 new materials from that list in 17 days.

Still unsolved

Problems still waiting for someone

Some of humanity's biggest problems are chemistry problems in disguise. They are waiting for a better molecule.

  1. Fertilizer without fossil fuels

    About half of the world's food depends on fertilizer made with a hundred-year-old process that burns huge amounts of natural gas. Making it cleanly, maybe right on the farm, would change how eight billion people eat.

  2. Artificial photosynthesis

    Plants turn sunlight, water and CO₂ into fuel. We still can't do that efficiently. Whoever builds an artificial leaf that works at scale turns the gas that heats the planet back into something useful.

  3. A battery ten times better

    Electric cars are here, but electric planes and ships need batteries that hold far more energy for their weight. That jump needs a chemistry nobody has found yet.

The treasure map

Chapter III · Biology

The Living Machine

Right now, inside you, millions of tiny machines are tying themselves into knots. For fifty years, nobody could say how.

Inside every cell of your body, millions of tiny machines are folding themselves into shape right now, while you read this. Each one is a chain of building blocks that knots itself into exactly the right form in a fraction of a second. For 50 years, nobody could predict that shape. Then an AI did, and its makers won a Nobel Prize.

Now scientists go one step further. They design brand-new proteins on a computer, ones nature never invented, and send them out to block viruses, neutralize snake venom and switch off disease. Biology has stopped being a subject you only watch. It has become something you build with.

Watch a protein fold. Then watch one go hunting.

A protein chain folds the way nature folds it. Then it is redesigned into a new protein that hunts down a virus-like target and locks onto its spike. Drag to turn it.

Why it's your kind of adventure

So much is still wide open here. Protein folding was a mystery yesterday and is a tool today. Tomorrow someone designs enzymes that eat plastic, or medicines that recognize exactly one cancer cell. In biology, what counts is the person who asks the new question. Creativity isn't a bonus here. It's the job.

True stories
  • Nobel Prize 2024

    Awarded for AlphaFold, the AI that predicts how proteins fold. And to David Baker, who designs proteins that never existed in nature.

  • 200 million

    Protein structures predicted by AlphaFold. Nearly every known protein on Earth.

  • 1 letter

    In 2025, a baby in Philadelphia was treated with a gene correction designed in a few months just for him. A single wrong letter in his DNA was rewritten.

Still unsolved

Problems still waiting for someone

Biology has the most personal problems on this list. Each one is measured in human lives.

  1. Beating superbugs

    Bacteria are learning to survive our antibiotics. Resistant infections already kill more than a million people a year, and almost no new kinds of antibiotics have been found in decades.

  2. Growing new organs

    More than 100,000 people in the US alone are waiting for an organ transplant, and many die waiting. Printing or growing a working kidney from a patient's own cells would end those lists.

  3. Stopping Alzheimer's

    Tens of millions of people are slowly losing their memories, and we still don't fully understand why. Whoever cracks it gives families back their grandparents.

Chapter IV · Robotics

The Machines Learn to Work

For fifty years they stayed behind fences. Now they are coming out.

For fifty years, factory robots were strong, fast and a little dumb. They lived behind fences and repeated the same move millions of times. Move one screw two centimeters, and someone had to reprogram them for a week.

That's ending. Robots are starting to learn instead of being programmed, the same way AI learned to write. Thousands of virtual copies practice in a simulation, fall over a million times, and pass what they learned to the real machine. They've started working next to people on car assembly lines. Warehouses are full of them. And the first humanoid helpers for the home are taking orders. The robot that folds your laundry is not science fiction anymore. It's a startup.

This chapter is close to my own world. Every day I watch goods move between factories, warehouses and stores. The next big change in that chain will have hands.

Hundreds of virtual copies of one robot try to walk at the same time. They stumble and fall, and every fall teaches all of them. Then one steps out into the real world. Tap to give it a push. It catches itself.

Why it's your kind of adventure

Robotics is the perfect field for someone who builds. It's physics, electronics, software and design in one machine you can actually touch. And because every robot has to work in the messy real world, where cups are slippery and nothing sits exactly where it should, the details decide everything.

True stories
  • Years in hours

    In simulation, thousands of copies of a robot practice at the same time. They collect years of experience in a few hours, and then the skill is loaded into the real robot.

  • Spartanburg

    Humanoid robots from the startup Figure have done trial work at BMW's car plant in South Carolina, placing metal parts with their own hands.

  • 2025

    The company 1X opened orders for NEO, a humanoid robot built to help around the house.

Still unsolved

Problems still waiting for someone

The hardest robotics problems aren't in the factory. They're in the real, messy world.

  1. Care for an aging world

    By 2050, about one in six people on Earth will be over 65. There won't be nearly enough caregivers. Robots that can help gently and safely, every day, may be the only way to let people grow old at home.

  2. Going where humans can't

    Cleaning up the damaged Fukushima reactors will take decades, because people can't work inside. Disaster zones, the deep sea and burning buildings all need robots that can think for themselves.

  3. Building on the Moon and Mars

    Nobody will ship whole cities into space. Robots will have to arrive first and build shelters out of the local dust and rock before people come.

Chapter V · Energy

The Fire of Stars

There is a piece of the Sun in northern Germany. It is held in place by nothing but magnets.

Everything in the chapters before needs power. Every data center, every robot, every electric car. AI alone is on its way to using more electricity than many whole countries. Whoever can deliver that power, clean and cheap, holds the keys to everything else.

In Greifswald, on Germany's Baltic coast, stands a machine that looks like a twisted metal doughnut. Inside it, a gas hotter than the core of the Sun floats in a vacuum, held in place by nothing but magnets. It's called Wendelstein 7-X, and it's practice for the day we light small stars on Earth.

Look inside.

Inside a stellarator like Wendelstein 7-X. Thousands of plasma particles race along twisted magnetic field lines and never touch the walls. Drag to turn it.

Why it's your kind of adventure

Energy is for people who want to build things that actually run, at full scale, in real life. It takes physics, chemistry and engineering at once. That's why it sits at the top of the ladder.

True stories
  • 43 seconds

    A 2025 world record at Wendelstein 7-X. That's how long it held its plasma at record performance.

  • 2022

    For the first time, a fusion reaction released more energy than the lasers put in. At the National Ignition Facility in California.

  • 100 hours

    That's how long an iron-air battery from Form Energy can deliver power. One is being built to back up a Google data center in Minnesota.

From the lab to the grid

A battery that breathes rust

While it discharges, iron rusts and powers a data center. While wind and sun charge it, the rust turns back into iron.

Still unsolved

Problems still waiting for someone

Energy problems are the biggest ones of all. Every other chapter depends on them.

  1. Fusion on the grid

    Scientists have shown that fusion works. Nobody has built a power plant that runs day after day and sells electricity. Whoever does gives the world almost limitless clean power.

  2. Power for 700 million people

    Around 700 million people still have no electricity at all. No light to read by at night, no fridge for medicine. Cheap, clean power that works far from any grid would change their lives overnight.

  3. Cooling without heating

    As the planet warms, the world could need about three times as much cooling by 2050. Today's air conditioners burn huge amounts of power and make the problem worse. We need a new way to stay cool.

Before you decide

Go deep in the subject that makes you forget dinner. Keep your eyes open to the other four. The future gets built where they meet.

And try it out before you decide. Your own reaction will tell you more than any trend analysis, mine included.

Ways to try it
  • Taster days. Many universities run a Schnupperstudium, for example KIT, RWTH Aachen and TU München. Sit in on a real lecture.
  • A summer in a lab. Max Planck, Fraunhofer and Helmholtz institutes take school students for a few weeks of internship.
  • A founder evening. Go to one startup event at a university founder center. Listen to how people pitch their inventions.
  • A counter-test. Spend a day on something completely different too. The contrast shows you fast where time disappears.

In the end, it's not the years in your life that count. It's the life in your years.
